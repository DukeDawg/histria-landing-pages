The Story of Carol Kennicott

There is a particular oblivion reserved for books that succeed too well. Main Street gave the American language a phrase, and the phrase promptly ate the book. Say the words today and people will nod — small towns, church suppers, narrow minds, a certain flatness of spirit — and not one in fifty will have read a page of it. The title has outlived the novel. We propose to reverse the arrangement.

The Book

Carol Milford is a librarian in St. Paul with a head full of civic beauty and no particular place to put it. She marries Dr. Will Kennicott, a decent country physician, and follows him home to Gopher Prairie, Minnesota — three thousand souls, a railway station, a row of false-fronted shops facing each other across the mud.

She arrives determined to make the town lovely. The town, it turns out, is not interested in being made lovely. What follows is the long, funny, wearing account of a woman trying to reform a place that has already decided what it is, and of the place quietly reforming her instead.

Lewis put his warning in a prefatory note, so that no reader could later claim to have been ambushed:

This is America — a town of a few thousand, in a region of wheat and corn and dairies and little groves. The town is, in our tale, called “Gopher Prairie, Minnesota.” But its Main Street is the continuation of Main Streets everywhere.

He knew the ground. Gopher Prairie is Sauk Centre, Minnesota, where Harry Sinclair Lewis was born in 1885 — the doctor’s son who read too much and did not fit. The novel is usually called a satire, and it is one; but it is a satire written by a man who could never quite stop loving the thing he was flaying. That contradiction is why the book survives, and why it still stings.

What surprises the first-time reader is the beauty. Lewis had an ear like a phonograph — the gossip, the boosterism, the terrible jokes at the Jolly Seventeen — but he could also write a sentence like the one that opens the book:

On a hill by the Mississippi where Chippewas camped two generations ago, a girl stood in relief against the cornflower blue of Northern sky.

That girl is Carol, and the whole novel is contained in her — a person standing against a sky, briefly, before the prairie closes in.

The Quarrel

Harcourt, Brace and Howe published Main Street on the twenty-third of October, 1920. It sold one hundred and eighty thousand copies in a little over six months, and something near two million Americans had read it before Lewis published another book. It was the best-selling novel in the country in 1921. It was also resented — the public library at Alexandria, Minnesota, declined to shelve it — and the resentment was rather the point.

Then came the affair Lewis never quite forgave. The Pulitzer jury chose Main Street for the 1921 prize. The trustees of Columbia University overruled them, invoking the clause requiring a novel that presented “the wholesome atmosphere of American life,” and gave the award instead to Edith Wharton’s The Age of Innocence.

What is less often told is what Wharton did next. She wrote to Lewis, and she was not gracious about her own prize. “When I discovered that I was being rewarded — by one of our leading Universities — for uplifting American morals,” she told him, “I confess I did despair.” On learning that the award should have been his, and had been withdrawn because his book had “offended a number of prominent persons in the Middle West,” she added that “disgust was added to despair.” The winner thought the prize misawarded, and said so to the man it had been taken from.

Lewis called it the Main Street burglary. In 1926 he was awarded the Pulitzer for Arrowsmith and refused it, citing the earlier theft. Four years after that he became the first American writer to receive the Nobel Prize in Literature, cited for his “vigorous and graphic art of description” — an honor he did not decline.

Why We Have Reprinted It

Because everyone knows the title and almost nobody has read past the third chapter. Because the quarrel Carol Kennicott started — between the place that made you and the person you meant to become — has not been settled anywhere in America in the hundred and six years since. And because a novel this good deserves better than to survive as a figure of speech.

There is a coda that Lewis would have enjoyed. Sauk Centre, which did not care at all for being made famous this way, long ago made its peace with its most difficult son. The town now calls its principal thoroughfare the Original Main Street, and the road that crosses it is Sinclair Lewis Avenue. The book won the argument by outlasting it.

The Introduction

The volume opens with a new introduction by Dr. A.K. Brackob, and it is not the usual scholarly preface. It takes Lewis at his method rather than his reputation: the maps he drew of Gopher Prairie before writing a line of it, marking every shop and the distance from the railroad to the better houses; the notebooks of overheard speech; the phrases — “common sense,” “practicality,” “the way things are done” — that the town keeps about it as moral shields. What later readers took for bluntness, it argues, was a refusal to soften the picture. Lewis was not trying to be subtle. He was trying to be exact.

It also makes the case that the novel has outlived its setting. The mechanism Lewis described — belonging as the local currency, difference tolerated briefly and then quietly neutralized, reputation adjusted downward without anyone raising a voice — is what one character in the book calls the Village Virus, and it did not stay in Minnesota.

And it ends, as introductions rarely do, with an admission: that its author grew up in a small Midwestern town with the same register, and recognized Gopher Prairie from the inside. Like the introductions to IT and Clara Bow: My Story before it, it is written as narrative. It is meant to be read, not consulted.

How This Book Was Made

The volume runs to 508 pages at a trim of six by nine inches (152 × 229 mm). The text is set in eleven-point Adobe Garamond Pro and printed in black on a cream book paper of 90 grams, chosen for its bulk and for its kindness to the eye over a long novel. An eight-page gathering is printed in four colors on a 170-gram coated matt stock and carries photographs of Lewis, the original 1920 dust jacket, and lobby cards, stills, and posters from the 1923 film. The endpapers are printed in four colors on 120-gram offset.

That last is not a small thing. Harry Beaumont’s Main Street — Florence Vidor as Carol, Monte Blue as Kennicott — opened at Warner Bros. in April 1923. The studio junked the negative in December 1948, and no print is known to survive. What is reproduced here is very nearly all that is left of it.

The binding is a Wibalin Buckram faux cloth over three-millimeter boards, printed in four colors and stamped in foil, with a squared spine, head-and-tail bands, and a red ribbon marker bound in. The sheets are sewn in signatures, as they ought to be.

Adobe Garamond Pro is the house standard across the series. The edition is limited to one thousand copies, and the volume is designated Forgotten Classics II.
